Most business owners who tell us they need a new CRM are frustrated with the one they have, and fair enough. The team doesn't use it, the reports don't match reality and half the work still happens in spreadsheets and inboxes. Switching feels like a fresh start.

But a new CRM set up the same way as the old one will end up in the same place within a year. Before you replace anything, it's worth working out whether the problem is the platform or the way it was set up. They need very different fixes.

## Is it the setup or the platform?

In our experience, most "bad CRMs" are perfectly good platforms with a bad setup. The software was configured quickly, around someone's best guess at how the business worked, and nobody has touched it since. Meanwhile the business changed.

Here's a quick way to tell them apart.

**Signs it's the setup:**

- **Fields nobody fills in.** Forms are cluttered with fields from the original rollout that no longer mean anything, so people skip them all, including the important ones.
- **Stages that don't match how you sell.** The pipeline says "Qualified, Proposal, Negotiation", but your team actually goes "Site visit, Quote, Follow-up". So deals sit in the wrong stage and the forecast is fiction.
- **Duplicates everywhere.** The same customer appears three times because web enquiries, imports and manual entry all create new records.
- **No automation, or the wrong automation.** Reminders that nobody acts on, emails that go out at the wrong time, or follow-ups that depend on someone remembering.
- **Nothing connects.** Leads from the website are typed in by hand, and accepted quotes are retyped into Xero.

Every one of these can be fixed inside the platform you already pay for, usually far faster and with much less disruption than moving.

**Signs it's the platform:**

- **It can't model how your business works.** You sell configured products, manage jobs across several sites or run quoting with supplier price lists, and the CRM has no sensible place to put any of it.
- **You've hit a hard limit.** Custom objects, automation rules, API calls or user permissions are capped in a way an upgrade won't solve.
- **The integrations you need don't exist.** Your accounting, inventory or job management software simply won't talk to it, and building a bridge would be fragile.
- **It's built for a different kind of business.** A CRM designed for high-volume online sales will always fight a business that runs on site visits and custom quotes, and the reverse is just as true.
- **The vendor has stopped investing.** Features are being retired, support is slow and the product hasn't meaningfully changed in years.

If most of your frustrations are on the first list, fix the setup. If they're on the second, it's time to look at other options.

## How to fix the setup

A CRM clean-up follows the same steps as a good first implementation, which is often the step that got skipped the first time.

1. **Map how work actually happens today.** Sit with the people doing the selling, quoting and follow-up. Write down every step, every handover and every place information gets typed in.
2. **Cut it back.** Remove fields, stages and automations nobody uses. A CRM with fewer fields that are always filled in beats one with lots of fields that are mostly empty.
3. **Rebuild the pipeline around your real stages.** Name them the way your team talks, and make the move from one stage to the next mean something specific.
4. **Clean the data.** Merge duplicates, archive dead records and fix the fields your reports depend on. Then stop new duplicates at the door with matching rules on web forms and imports.
5. **Connect the obvious things.** Website forms straight into the CRM. Accepted quotes into your accounting software. Ad leads tagged with their source, so you can see which marketing actually brings in customers (our [tracking and reporting](/services/tracking-and-reporting) work picks up from there).
6. **Train people on the new version.** Show the team what changed and why it's less work for them. If the CRM doesn't save them time, they won't use it.

## How to replace a CRM without losing everything

If the platform really is the problem, a migration is worth doing properly. The most common mistakes are rushing the data move and recreating the old setup in the new tool.

- **Choose on fit, not features.** Shortlist platforms against your mapped process, not a feature checklist. Most CRMs tick the same boxes; what matters is how naturally your way of working fits.
- **Decide what's worth bringing across.** Active customers, open deals and the history you actually look at. Years of stale leads can often be archived to a file rather than migrated.
- **Clean before you move.** Migrating messy data just gives you a more expensive mess in a new system.
- **Run both for a short overlap.** A week or two with the old system read-only gives the team a safety net without letting them keep working in it.
- **Plan the integrations up front.** Website forms, accounting, email and ad platforms all need reconnecting. Map each one before go-live, not after something breaks.
- **Make sure you own it.** The new CRM should be in your accounts, with your data and documentation, so you're never locked in to whoever set it up.

## Questions to ask before you decide

If you're still unsure, these questions usually settle it:

- Could we describe exactly what the CRM should do differently, step by step?
- Have we ever had someone review and rebuild the setup since it went live?
- Is the problem that the CRM *can't* do something, or that nobody set it up to?
- What would we lose by moving: history, integrations, team habits?
- If we fixed the three biggest frustrations, would we still want to leave?

If the answer to the last one is "no", you probably need a clean-up, not a new platform.
